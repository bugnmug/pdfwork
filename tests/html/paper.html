Measuring Reading Order in Converted Documents

A. Rao and K. Menon

Department of Computer Science, Example University

Abstract. Documents converted from PDF often lose the order in which their text should be read, especially when pages are set in two columns. We describe a simple method that finds the columns on each page and reads them in turn, and we measure how often the result matches the reading order a person would choose. On a set of three hundred papers the method agrees with human readers on ninety-four percent of pages.

1 Introduction

Portable documents describe where each character sits on a page but say little about the order in which the characters should be read. A converter must recover that order before it can produce text that flows correctly in an editor.1

The problem is easy for a single column of text and hard for layouts with several columns, sidebars and figures. Earlier systems relied on the order in which characters appear in the file, which often follows the order in which the authoring tool drew them rather than the order of reading.

In this paper we focus on the most common difficult case, a page set in two columns with a title and abstract across its full width.

2 Method

We group characters into lines and lines into blocks, then look for a vertical gap that runs down most of the page. Blocks to the left of the gap are read before blocks to its right. Where a block spans the gap, such as a title, it is read first.

Lines whose ends line up with the column edges are treated as running text; short lines that end a sentence close a paragraph. Each paragraph is then joined back into a single block of text.

3 Results

Table 1 shows the agreement for each kind of layout. Pages with a single column were almost always read correctly. Two-column pages reached ninety-four percent, with most errors caused by figures placed across both columns.

Mixed layouts, which combine columns of different widths on one page, remain the hardest case and are the subject of further work.
